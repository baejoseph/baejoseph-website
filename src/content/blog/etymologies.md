I published three books in quick succession.

My first three commercial books ever, with my name on the cover. (Not counting my [doctorate dissertation](https://www.mathgenealogy.org/id.php?id=203936).)

Earlier this year I built [three typing games](/typing-games). Now, three books. They say buses come in threes. I did not plan a convoy — I planned one booklet for my own children during a long summer, and the thing multiplied.

## Classical Etymologies — Greek

[Amazon US](https://a.co/d/0eL7ktWR) · [Amazon UK](https://amzn.eu/d/0dW6uoNX)

Thirty-one Greek roots, one a day. Each day takes a root like *bio* or *chron*, builds real English words out of it with the child's own hands, and then matches it to the Korean 한자어 that carries the same idea.

## Classical Etymologies — Latin

[Amazon US](https://a.co/d/07qWJpuK) · [Amazon UK](https://amzn.eu/d/04LY7Lud)

The same treatment for thirty-one Latin roots — *vid*, *scrib*, *dict*, *duc*, *fac*, *ven*, *spect* — plus the phrases still printed on our walls: *veni, vidi, vici*; *vox populi*; *status quo*; *facta non verba*. It also explains why September is not the seventh month, which no one has ever asked me but everyone should.

## Biblical Etymologies — Hebrew

[Amazon US](https://a.co/d/04wDbMzM) · [Amazon UK](https://amzn.eu/d/0eShOLWH)

Thirty-one Hebrew roots from the Old Testament, taught the way Hebrew actually works: three consonants first, then the vowels and the little bits that change the job, then the whole family of words sharing that skeleton — *bara*, *mayim*, *ruach*, *qadosh*, *shalom*, *shema*, *yasha*, *bayit*.

## How They Started

Not with a business plan. With two sets of children on two continents.

My own children are in secondary school here in the United Kingdom. Their English is strong; their Korean gets weaker every year. My nephews and nieces moved to California, where they attend a Classical Academy — Latin, Greek roots, the classical bones of English — and they already know a good deal of Hanja.

I watched both sets of children and thought: what if we joined the dots? A Greek root on one side, the 한자어 with the same meaning on the other, and the child suddenly sees one word-building system doing its work in two languages at once. Hospital signs a child walks past every week stop being a wall of syllables and become readable.

That became the Greek volume, and then the Latin one beside it. Thirty-one days, one root and one verse a day: small enough to finish, big enough to matter.

## The Hebrew One Came From Somewhere Else

During the pandemic I sat at home with my then seven-year-old son, Joshua (준수), and we set ourselves a strange piece of work: memorising all 176 verses of Psalm 119 — in Korean. Twenty-two stanzas of eight verses each. Twenty-two, because the psalm is an acrostic and the Hebrew alphabet has twenty-two letters, one stanza per letter, alef to tav. By the time we could recite the psalm, we had memorised the entire 22-letter alef-bet that it is built on. So began my personal passion and hobby of biblical Hebrew words (roots).

This Hebrew book is hopefully a beginning. Same shape as the Classical Etymologies books — one root a day, one verse a day — but this time the roots are the ones the Old Testament itself is built on. This is personally the book I'm most excited about: I've been looking for a book like this myself, but haven't found one. So I made it myself. Thank You, Jesus!

## The Rule I Would Not Break

Every day in these books opens with Scripture, and the verses were chosen by a rule I refused to bend: Psalm 119 first, wherever a real match existed; another psalm or a proverb if not; the rest of the Bible only when the match was exact. Not decoration. The [fear of the Lᴏʀᴅ](/fear) is the beginning of knowledge, and I did not want a book about words to teach my children that words come first.

*Soli Deo Gloria.*
