A boat, eight, and a person. That is **船**, the character for ship: 舟 the boat, 八 the eight, 口 the mouth that makes a man. **Eight souls went into the ark, and the character still says so** (1 Peter 3:20).

I did not find that. Pastor **C. H. Kang** did, in a footnote to a missionary’s Mandarin textbook. He set 造 beside Genesis 2:7—**dust, a mouth, and a man on his feet**—and then spent **forty years** asking what the other characters had kept. *Genesis and the Chinese* came out in Hong Kong in 1950. *The Discovery of Genesis*, with Ethel R. Nelson, in 1979.

## The covers were pointing here

Three volumes went up in September: Greek, Latin, Hebrew. Look closely at the texture behind those titles. It is **a drift of roots**—and already among them are Chinese characters and Hangul, 天 and 地, 光 and 造. **The Chinese was in the design months before the Chinese books existed.** **A watermark was doing what a watermark is for**: marking a page that had not been written yet.

**These two are that page.**

## Volume 1 — the door

**155 characters, five a day, thirty-one days**, about ten minutes each, from **the first morning of Genesis to the gospel**. **You need no Chinese to begin.** Every part of every character is named on the card, so a child builds the word with his own eyes.

![Inside Volume 1: the Day 2 spread on the character 學, school — a verse, the parts of the character, and five cards with pinyin, Korean and Japanese](/assets/etymologies-2-vol1-spread.jpg)

*Day 2 of Volume 1. 學 is a child, 子, under a cover, with two hands lifting it off—and Genesis 1:31, Proverbs 22:15 and Matthew 11:29 standing beside the strokes.*

The 學 page does the work I hoped for. God saw everything He had made, and indeed it was very good (Genesis 1:31). Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child (Proverbs 22:15). The cover inside the character is **that foolishness**, and **our own hands cannot lift it**; then Matthew 11:29, take My yoke upon you and learn from Me, and one line under it: *Jesus takes the yoke, and He teaches the child.*

**Ten minutes. Five characters.** That is the whole day.

## Volume 2 — the story

One character a day through Genesis 1–11, and one four-character saying told as the story it is: 塞翁之馬, the old man at the border, the horse that ran away, the horse that came home, the broken leg, the war.

![Inside Volume 2: the Day 12 spread on 婪, greedy — Genesis 3:6 in English and Korean, stroke order, a word lab and the idiom of the day](/assets/etymologies-2-vol2-spread.jpg)

*Day 12 of Volume 2. 婪 is 林, a grove, over 女, a woman: Genesis 3:6 in one character. The same grove spells 禁, the ban, four days earlier.*

**婪 is greedy, wanting**: a grove above, a woman below, the woman of Genesis 3:6 standing under the trees looking at what she was told not to take. The same 林 carries 禁, the ban, on day 8—**one grove spells the command and the craving.** And in the margin, a note that the old dictionaries filed many bad words under the woman radical: **a habit of the scribes, not a verdict on women**. That kind of care is on every page.

## One character, three countries

**The Chinese volumes were the hard ones.** A character is not one thing: 學 is 학 in Seoul, がく in Tokyo, xué in Beijing, and the mainland writes 学. Print the wrong form and you have handed a Korean child a character he cannot find in the book he already owns.

So the page prints **the traditional form, the one Korea and Taiwan kept**, with the simplified form in grey underneath where the mainland differs. It gives the pinyin with the tone, the Korean reading, and one Japanese on and one kun. Hanja, kanji, hanzi: **one writing system, four scripts, and a child standing in the middle of all of them.** **Getting that right took longer than any Greek or Latin page I have written.** I would not have published these two at all if I could not make them compelling as well as correct.

## Six years ago

The Hebrew came out of one summer. **Joshua was seven.** We sat at home in the pandemic and set out to memorise all 176 verses of Psalm 119 in Korean—twenty-two stanzas of eight verses, one stanza for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, alef to tav. **By the time we could recite the psalm, we had memorised the alphabet it is built on**, and I had fallen in love with the languages God actually used.

**Six years later: five courses, four languages**, and one book on a bench outside a shop.

> “Oh, how I love Your law! It is my meditation all the day. You, through Your commandments, make me wiser than my enemies, for they are ever with me. I have more understanding than all my teachers, for Your testimonies are my meditation. I understand more than the ancients, because I keep Your precepts.”
>
> Psalm 119:97–100 (NKJV)

I have degrees from **Cambridge and Yale**. **The psalm has made me wiser than all of it.** That is not modesty and it is not a boast. It is the plain report of six years of one chapter, recited in the car and at the table, until it was not a text I knew but a text that knew me.

Every day of these books opens on a verse, chosen the way it was in [the first three volumes](/etymologies). **A book about words must not teach a child that words come first.**

I believe we are living at the end of the last days. I want the next generation of the Body of Christ found faithful and watchful, like the five wise virgins, **with oil in their lamps—and the oil real**.

Maranatha.
