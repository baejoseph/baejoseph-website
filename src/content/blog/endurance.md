Sir Ernest Shackleton would not smile until he had counted the heads.

I cannot let that scene go. August 1916. A Chilean tug called *Yelcho* has just broken a hole in the Weddell pack. On that sea and that ice, the window for a rescue is about one hour. That day, on the black beach of Elephant Island, twenty-two of his crew have been waiting 128 days since their captain left them in a lifeboat. The words he left them were these: “Hold on. I will come back. And be ready to leave at any time.”

He counted through binoculars first. Every man answered, “All well.” Then he smiled. Then, before the pack could close, every man was aboard, and they were gone from Elephant Island.

Not one of the twenty-eight *Endurance* men died.

**He promised he would come back. The sea was against him for months. When the opening came, it lasted an hour. The men who were packed lived.**

That is the church. That is us. That is the [pre-tribulation rapture](/rapture).

Sir Ernest Shackleton led the *Endurance* expedition (1914–17). The aim was to cross Antarctica. Ice crushed her in the Weddell Sea. She sank in November 1915. The men lived on the floes, then reached uninhabited Elephant Island on 15 April 1916 — first dry land in 497 days. No ships passed there. They had no radio.

So on 24 April, Shackleton left in the lifeboat *James Caird*. Five men with him: navigator Frank Worsley, Tom Crean, carpenter Harry McNish, Timothy McCarthy, John Vincent. The other twenty-two stayed under second-in-command Frank Wild, with the order: “Be ready to leave at any time.” Miraculously, Shackleton and the five crossed eight hundred miles of the Drake Passage in sixteen days and reached South Georgia on 10 May. After a few days’ rest ashore, with no map, Shackleton, Worsley and Crean walked thirty-six hours across the range of Mount Paget (2,935 metres) and on 20 May reached the Stromness whaling station. Twenty-six days after they had left Elephant Island, it was their first contact with civilisation.

As he had promised, Sir Ernest came back for them with rescue ships. The first three were blocked by ice. The fourth was *Yelcho*, Captain Luis Pardo. She broke through on 30 August 1916 — 128 days after the *Caird* left, 137 days after they first landed. That day too, the men on the beach had their kit packed. They were ready to move in that same hour. Shackleton later said that if they had not been ready, they would not have got out.

Jesus did not leave us a “maybe.” He left us a promise.

>

He will come again. Personally. To receive us to Himself. Surely. Soon. Suddenly.

> “For the Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first. Caught up. *Harpazō.* In a moment. The dead rise first; then we who are alive and remain. Pre-tribulation rapture is not a hobby chart. It is the bodily resurrection of the saints, before the wrath of God that will be poured out on the whole world. “For God did not appoint us to wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9). The pattern is older than Thessalonians. The righteous are removed first. Noah before the flood. Lot before the fire. Then judgment falls.

The opening will not linger. Shackleton’s hour on the ice is the picture. Paul says:

> “Behold, I tell you a mystery: We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed—in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality.”
>
> 1 Corinthians 15:51–53

In the twinkling of an eye. A moment. If the kit is not packed, you miss the hour. That is why [imminency](/jubilee) is the engine of holy living. Live like Paul. Live like the early church. Keep the [appointments](/feasts) as rehearsal, and keep the bag packed even on an ordinary Tuesday.
